A causal history of a simple machine

The helix that holds the world together

The screw thread is almost as foundational as the wheel, but it comes after it in the causal chain: the wheel is one of its dependencies. A helix is an inclined plane wrapped around a cylinder. You cannot have one until you already have rotary geometry and the wedge. Pick up an ordinary stainless bolt and that whole stack is in your hand: ISO-metric, rolled not cut, its grandparents an olive press and a clock.

01 — causal order

Why the wheel had to come first

Technology is a stack. Each layer is illegal until the one below it exists. The wheel gives you rotation. The axle constrains it. The cylinder is the geometry of that rotation. The inclined plane is a different primitive: the wedge, the ramp, the chisel. Wrap the plane around the cylinder and you get a helix. That is the screw. It could not have come first.

0
Nature already helical

Vines, tendrils, snail shells, the twist of a growing stem. Romance words for the screw come from Latin vītis, vine: French vis, Italian vite.

1
Wheel & axle

Constrained rotation. Without this, there is no cylinder worth wrapping anything around, and no rotary input to convert.

2
Inclined plane

The other primitive. A small force over a long distance becomes a large force over a short one. Wedge, ramp, ploughshare.

3
Helix / screw

The plane, wrapped. Rotation becomes translation. A shallow pitch plus friction becomes a lock: the fastener.

4
Precision screw

The helix as a measuring instrument. Micrometer, dividing engine, leadscrew. Length from angle.

5
The screw that cuts screws

A leadscrew on a lathe cuts more screws, including better leadscrews. Machine tools are a hall of mirrors of threads.

6
Standard as social technology

Whitworth, Sellers, ISO. A physical invention is not a civilisation-scale one until any nut fits any bolt of its name.

“The screw was one of the last of the simple machines to be invented.” Standard claim in the history of mechanisms — and it is true in a specific sense: last because hardest. A helix is not a shape you stumble into with a hammer.

China, which independently produced the other classical simple machines, did not invent the screw. Jesuit missionaries brought it in the seventeenth century (Hydraulic Methods of the Far West, 1612). Joseph Needham treated this as a genuine absence, not a gap in the record. The helix is not obvious. That is the point.

Two properties fall out of the geometry, and they fork the whole later history:

motion

Rotation → translation

Turn the shaft, the nut advances. Water climbs, a press closes, a lead screw feeds a tool. Pitch is a gear ratio made of metal.

fastening

Self-locking

If the pitch is shallow enough, friction at the flanks defeats back-driving. The joint stays done up. That is a bolt. It is why you can torque something down and walk away, and expect it to still be tight next year.

02 — what a thread is

Pitch, flanks, and a lock that is also a motor

Pitch is the axial distance from crest to crest. Lead is how far the screw advances in one turn. For a single-start thread they are the same. Mechanical advantage is circumference divided by lead: a fine thread is a long ramp, and a long ramp is a strong one. It is also a lock. Make the ramp too steep (a bottle cap, a multi-start lid) and the helix wants to undo itself. That is a feature when you want the lid off. It is a disaster in a bolt meant to stay tight.

The thread angle is the included angle of the V. Fasteners like 60°: wide roots, strong, plenty of friction. Power screws (jacks, vices, lathe leadscrews) want less friction, so they went square, then Acme at 29°. Pipe threads add a taper so the flanks themselves make the seal.

Handedness is a convention with a muscle underneath it. A right-handed person tightening a right-hand screw uses the stronger supinator of the forearm. Most people are right-handed, so most threads are right-handed. The exceptions are the interesting ones: the left pedal of a bicycle, a gas fitting that must not mate with an oxygen line, a turnbuckle that tightens both ways at once.

Two jobs, two profiles

The same helix, tuned. Friction is wanted in a bolt and fought in a jack.

Sharper V cuts and locks. Trapezoid bears load and can be recut as it wears. Romans already had both V and square threads; they just could not make them cheap.

03 — the steps

A timeline, as far back as it will go

The honest version of “who invented the screw” is that nobody did, in the Edison sense. A geometry was noticed, then used for water, then for pressure, then for holding two things together, then for measuring, then for making more of itself, then for making all of those interchangeable. Several of the earliest names are traditional. They are marked.

motion fastening precision / machine tool standard
before the record

A shape that grows

nature

The vine is already a screw

A climbing plant traces a helix around a stem because that is how a tip can advance while keeping contact. Snail shells and horns do the same in three dimensions. You do not need to invent the helix. You need to notice it, then cut it on purpose, in a material that is not alive.

c. 700 BCE – 250 BCE

Water, then theory

c. 700 BCE

Sennacherib’s bronze screws? disputed

Stephanie Dalley reads a Neo-Assyrian inscription of Sennacherib (r. 704–681 BCE) as describing huge bronze water-raising screws for the terraced garden at Nineveh — which would put the water screw centuries before Archimedes, and in metal. John Peter Oleson is unconvinced: the words do not unambiguously mean “screw,” and there is no archaeological machine. If Dalley is right, the hanging gardens of later Greek memory were at Nineveh, not Babylon, and the screw is Mesopotamian. Treat as an open case, not a foundation.

c. 400 BCE

Archytas of Tarentum traditional

Pythagorean, general, friend of Plato, sometimes called the founder of mechanics. Later writers credit him with the screw. There is no surviving device and no contemporary description of a thread. What is solid: by the fourth century BCE, Greek mathematics had the helix as an object of thought, and southern Italy was a place where thought became machines. Archytas is a plausible ceiling on “the Greeks already had the idea,” not a patent.

c. 250 BCE

Archimedes: water, spirals, the first theory

The water screw — a helix inside a cylinder, rotated, lifting water — is the first screw we can actually talk about. Diodorus says Archimedes invented it in Egypt. Posidonius implies he saw Egyptians already using one. Oleson’s conservative position: no literary or archaeological evidence before c. 250 BCE, and the device bears Archimedes’ name for a reason. He also wrote On Spirals. This is the moment the helix becomes a machine with a proof behind it. Egyptians and Romans used it for irrigation, bilge, and mine drainage. Vitruvius, two centuries later, still gives a carpenter’s recipe: eight helical channels, pitched at a 3-4-5 triangle, turned by the treading of men.

2nd century BCE – 2nd century CE

Presses, taps, and the first metal fasteners

1st–2nd c. BCE

The screw press

Oil and wine. A wooden V-thread, often enormous, turning against a nut in the frame, converting a man’s shoulders into a constant, controllable pressure that a lever-and-weight press cannot hold as cleanly. Hero describes the evolution of these presses; machines of the same family were still at work in the nineteenth century. A cloth press survived at Herculaneum. A twin-screw press is painted on a wall in Pompeii. Gutenberg will borrow this, unchanged in principle, to put inked type against paper.

1st c. CE

Hero of Alexandria: definition, and a tap

In the Mechanica the screw is one of five things that can “set a load in motion.” Hero states the geometry that this whole page is about: an inclined plane wrapped around a cylinder. He describes how to lay out and cut wooden screws, and a device for cutting an internal thread — a tap. Female threads are the hard part. A bolt you can file. A nut you have to invent a tool for. That tool is already in Alexandria in the first century.

before 79 CE

Roman metal threads: jewellery, surgery, a lamp, a wood screw

The eruption of Vesuvius is a dating stamp. A travelling lamp from Herculaneum has stacked cylinders joined by male V-threads. Surgical specula from Pompeii open on a metal screw, as they still do. Gold and bronze square threads survive on fibulae and bracelets in London, the Louvre, the Met, Berlin, the Getty. At Silchester, a pointed iron screw 157 mm long was found embedded in wood: a Roman wood screw, V-form, about 68° included angle. Sim and Legg’s reconstruction: a smith can forge and file one in about an hour. So the Romans could have made them in quantity. They mostly did not. Nails were cheaper, and a handmade helix is still a luxury. Fastening with a screw is understood. It is not yet a civilisation’s default joint.

3rd – 14th centuries

The long thin stretch

late antiquity → medieval

The idea does not die. It also does not spread.

Large wooden screws keep turning in presses — oil, wine, cloth, later paper. The water screw fades in much of Europe by the end of the third century and persists in Visigothic and Islamic Spain; Oleson suggests the Arabs may have brought it back after the eighth century. What does not happen is the thing we now think of as “screws”: a bag of identical metal fasteners. Cutting a helix by hand is still too expensive for that. The stack is waiting on machine tools, and machine tools are waiting on a better screw.

15th – 16th centuries

Printers, clocks, and a lathe that already knows the trick

c. 1440

Gutenberg does not invent the screw press. He aims it at type.

Movable type is the famous half. The other half is even pressure, over a plane, repeatable. That is a screw press, already ancient, already used on grapes, olives, cloth, and paper. Gutenberg’s own press does not survive. The earliest picture of a printing press is 1499. Wooden screws give way to metal ones on presses around 1550. The information revolution is downstream of a wine-maker’s helix.

15th c.

Clockmakers make the fastener common

A clock is a pile of small, serviceable joints. You cannot rivet a movement you expect to take apart. Metal screws — still handmade, still precious — become the joint of precision work. Armourers and instrument makers follow. This is the real beginning of the screw as a fastener in the modern sense, a good century before anyone has a factory for them. Augers and drills, the helix as a way of eating wood and earth, start showing up in European paintings around the same time.

1483

The Hausbuch lathe: a master screw cutting a screw

Das Mittelalterliche Hausbuch shows, a little confusedly (the artist draws mixed handedness), a thread-cutting machine with a leadscrew and slide. It is the earliest picture we have of the idea that will build the industrial world: one screw, already believed, guiding the tool that cuts the next. Leonardo sketches screw-cutting lathes and change gears around the same generation, probably without building them. Jacques Besson publishes a screw-cutting lathe in 1569. The bootstrap has started, on paper and in a few workshops, three hundred years before Maudslay.

1600 – 1770

Theory, a new continent, and the first precision originals

1600

Galileo writes down the dynamics

Le Meccaniche treats the screw with the rest of the simple machines: ideal mechanical advantage, friction as the thing that makes the real machine diverge from the diagram. The lock and the motor are now a calculation, not just a craft rule.

1612

The screw arrives in China, as a book

Sabatino de Ursis and Xu Guangqi publish Hydraulic Methods of the Far West, with Vitruvius’s water screw drawn for a Chinese audience. Later Jesuit instrument-makers (Schreck, Wang Zheng, Verbiest) treat the screw as the sixth simple machine. It is used, where it is used, more for mine drainage than irrigation — Sado Island gold mines in Japan built them by the hundred. The geometry did not arise there. It travelled.

1760

Wyatt brothers: wood screws as a factory product

British patent 751, Job and William Wyatt of Staffordshire: head the blank, slot it, then cut the thread from a master (tail) screw that advances the spindle. Blunt-ended, parallel-thread iron wood screws, made by a machine. Their mill at Tatenhill was not a commercial triumph, but the direction is set. A wood screw is a self-tapper: it cuts its own nut in the timber. Pointed gimlet tips — so you do not have to drill first — wait until the 1830s–40s.

1770

Ramsden’s lathe: a small original, a long accurate screw

Jesse Ramsden, instrument maker, builds the first really satisfactory screw-cutting lathe and a dividing engine whose tangent worm is a precision screw. Fine threads are how you make sextants, micrometers, and the surveying instruments that lay out canals. The method is already recursive: cut a short screw as well as you can, then use it to cut a longer one. Error averaging is a philosophical idea you can hold in your hand.

1795 – 1841

The industrial screw, then the first law of threads

1800

Maudslay: the combination that stuck

Henry Maudslay does not invent the slide rest, the leadscrew, or change gears. Senot in France (1795) and David Wilkinson in Rhode Island (1798) are in the same water. What Maudslay builds is the industrially practical lathe: leadscrew geared to the spindle, tool clamped to a rest that slides on planed ways, change gears for pitch. He then does the social half of the invention inside his own shop: a set of taps and dies, so that any bolt of a given size fits any nut of that size. Interchangeable parts, for fasteners, as a workshop fact. His pupils include Joseph Whitworth and James Nasmyth. The machine-tool industry is a Maudslay family tree.

1834–1846

The gimlet point: a wood screw you can start with a push

Blunt machine-made screws still wanted a pilot hole. A threaded conical point — the gimlet — lets the screw start itself. Claims tangle among Sloat & Springsteen (US 1837), the Providence Screw Company (1834 is sometimes cited), Cullen Whipple, and J. T. Sloan’s 1846 patent, which specified a point whose thread continues the body pitch. This is the wood screw a carpenter would recognise. Thread rolling, which displaces metal instead of cutting it away, is tried from 1851 and becomes the way almost all fasteners are made in the next century. A rolled thread is stronger: the grain follows the helix.

1841

Whitworth collects the mongrels and picks 55°

Joseph Whitworth, Maudslay-trained, gathers sample screws from British workshops and proposes a law: 55° included angle, rounded crests and roots, and a defined number of threads per inch for each diameter. It becomes British practice in the 1860s, then the Empire’s. The Royal Navy can order an engine from more than one shop. This is the first national screw-thread standard on Earth. The rounded root is kinder to fatigue than a sharp V — a fact that will be borrowed, a century later, into the Unified thread.

1864 – 1948

A second standard, a war, a merger

1864

Sellers: 60°, flat, and easier to cut

William Sellers, at the Franklin Institute in Philadelphia, proposes a 60° thread with crests and roots flattened by one-eighth of the pitch. A 60° gauge is a geometry any mechanic can file; Whitworth’s 55° with radiused crests is not. Railroads and the US Navy take it up. It becomes the United States Standard, then National Coarse and Fine. Pipe threads fork here too: Britain’s BSP keeps Whitworth’s 55° (and often a taper to seal); America’s NPT takes Sellers’ 60° and tapers it. Two industrial civilisations, two angles, and a century of adaptors.

1876–1894

Metric Europe cannot agree with itself either

Switzerland’s Thury thread (47.5°, watchmaking, 1876). Germany’s Loewenherz (53° 8′). French and German 60° metric forms eventually dominate the continent. The fastener is now cheap. The argument is about which helix the cheap ones should be.

1894

Acme: a power thread that a lathe can actually cut

Albert Man Powell proposes a 29° trapezoidal thread to replace square threads on travelling parts — vices, jacks, leadscrews. Square threads are efficient and a nuisance to machine. Acme can be cut with a sensible tool, wears in a way you can take up with a split nut, and becomes the power-screw of the machine shop. (Metric countries later get a similar trapezoid as Tr / DIN 103.)

1907 / 1932

Robertson, then Phillips: the interface to the helix

P. L. Robertson’s square socket (Canada, 1907) cold-forms, holds the bit, and does not cam out. Henry Ford likes it and cannot get a licence on the terms he wants; North America outside Canada mostly does not get it. John P. Thompson’s cruciform patents of 1932, commercialised by Henry Phillips, do the opposite on purpose: they cam out when the screw is home, which is what a power driver on a 1930s car line wants. By 1940 most US screw makers are licensed. WWII is largely Phillips-assembled. The drive is not the thread. It is the last millimetre of the stack: how a human or a motor is allowed to touch the helix.

1948

Unified: the Allies get tired of not fitting

1914–18 made incompatible threads an operational problem. 1939–45 made it a worse one. In 1948 Britain, the United States and Canada agree the Unified Thread Standard: Sellers’ 60° and flat crest, Whitworth’s rounded root for fatigue. Inch countries now share a helix. Metric countries do not. The next fight is the last one.

1947 – now

A world thread, and the bolt in your hand

1958–1968

ISO metric: one profile, millimetres

ISO is founded in 1947. Technical Committee 1, Screw threads, is not a side project. ISO/R 68 (1958) sets the basic 60° profile; ISO/R 724 (1968) the metric diameters and pitches. Crests essentially flat, roots rounded, flanks at the angle Sellers picked because it was easy to file. The car industry outside North America, the building trades, and almost every piece of consumer hardware you can buy today speak this language: M8×1.25, M10×1.5. UNC and UNF remain in US customary and a lot of aerospace. The stack is now a standard you can look up.

1874 → 1950s

Ball screws: take the friction out

A conventional leadscrew wastes most of the input on sliding friction, which is also the lock. Put recirculating balls in the nut and you keep the helix’s conversion of rotation to translation, at >90% efficiency, and you can preload out the backlash. Patents from 1874; a Cleveland design in 1898; aircraft in WWII; GM’s Saginaw steering after the war; then every CNC machine. The fastener wants friction. The machine tool, by the twentieth century, wants the other fork.

1967 →

Torx, rolling, stainless, the unnoticed present

Camcar’s Torx (1967) is a six-lobe drive that neither cams out like Phillips nor waits for a licence like Robertson. Almost all modern fasteners are thread-rolled, not cut: faster, less scrap, fatigue-stronger. Stainless — chromium’s gift — lets a thread live outdoors. Property classes (8.8, 10.9, A2-70) turn a bag of bolts into an engineering specification. None of this is as famous as Archimedes. All of it is why the helix is now boring, which is the highest compliment a technology can be paid.

04 — recursion

You need a screw to make a better screw

This is the part that makes the screw different from the wheel. A wheel does not help you make a rounder wheel in the same way. A leadscrew does. Ramsden cuts a long screw from a short, careful original. Maudslay’s lathe, whose carriage is driven by a screw, cuts the next leadscrew. Whitworth, later, will scrape surface plates and make measuring machines whose micrometers are screws, so that the next screw can be held to a fraction of a thousandth. The machine-tool industry is this loop, industrialised. Steam engines, railways, clocks, guns with interchangeable locks, and eventually the mill that rolled the last bolt you tightened: all of them sit on a helix that was taught by an older helix.

The causal stack has a strange last step. Once the screw can reproduce itself more accurately than the last generation, accuracy is no longer a craft mystery. It is a breeding programme. That is why 1800 feels like a cliff in this history even though the Hausbuch already knew the move in 1483. Knowing the move is not the same as running it every day, on metal, for sale, in a shop whose nuts fit each other.

06 — an object in the hand

What is in an ordinary bolt

Take a stainless hex-flange bolt of the sort that holds outdoor hardware together: an M8 or M10 in A2 or A4 stainless, ISO metric coarse, often with a serrated flange in place of a lock washer. It is not an interesting object, which is the whole achievement. Unpacked, it is a timeline.

geometry · 1st c. CEHelix as inclined plane

Hero’s definition. Rotation of the nut (or the bolt) becomes clamp load. Pitch 1.25 mm on M8 is a shallow ramp: it locks.

job · Roman / 15th c.Fastener, not a press

The Romans knew this joint. Clockmakers made it ordinary. You are not pressing olives with it. You are holding two parts together, and you expect to be able to undo it years from now.

head · medieval →Hexagon

A wrenchable head is a separate invention from the thread. The flange spreads the load into the part beneath it and, if serrated, bites in as a lock.

profile · 1864 / 195860° ISO metric

Sellers’ angle, because a 60° gauge is easy to make and check. ISO’s millimetres. Not Whitworth’s 55°. If a Victorian British bolt turned up in the same box, it would not fit, and would be wrong in a way you could measure with a cheap thread gauge.

making · 1851 → 20th c.Rolled, not cut

The blank is squeezed between dies rather than machined. The grain follows the flanks, so it is stronger in fatigue than a cut thread, and cheap enough that nobody counts them. Wyatt’s factory, fulfilled.

material · 20th c.A2 / A4 stainless

A self-healing film of chromium oxide. A carbon-steel helix left outdoors is a brown smear in a few seasons. Stainless is why the ancient geometry can live outside, next to aluminium and copper, without turning into a battery and a stain.

standard · 1968A name that fits

M8×1.25 means the same thing in a factory in Taiwan, a distributor in Halifax, and a workbench in Nova Scotia. That sentence is the Whitworth project, completed. The physical invention was Hellenistic. The civilisation-scale invention is the name.

07 — the moral, if there is one

Boring is the goal

The wheel is famous because it looks like a beginning. The screw is not famous, even though a world of clocks, presses, machine tools, engines, micrometers, and bolted steel is downstream of it, because a good thread disappears into use. You only notice it when the nut from one shop will not take the bolt from another, or when a fastener strips because someone reached for the wrong driver.

The causal chain is the interesting part. No cylinder, no helix. No helix, no press. No press, no printed book of the European kind. No leadscrew, no interchangeable machine parts. No standard, no spare part. No cheap, reliable bolt, and no flat-pack anything you can assemble on a Saturday and trust in a gale. Every layer is still there, in the object. The vine, the wedge, the cylinder, Alexandria, the clockmaker, Maudslay’s taps, Sellers’ 60°, and a rolling die in a factory that will never know your name.
